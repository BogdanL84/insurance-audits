# Top 10 Scariest Insurance Policy Terms (SDV Law, 2018)

Attorney-sourced list of dangerous policy language found in the field. Use as conviction evidence at final meetings and as audit checklist items.

## 1. Complete Bodily Injury Exclusions
Subcontractor policies with BI exclusions for employees — especially in high-cost markets. If an injured employee sues an additional insured, the downstream policy won't respond. The AI's corporate program absorbs a loss that should have been transferred.

## 2. Privity of Contract in Additional Insured Endorsements
Language: "when you and such person or organization agree" — limits AI coverage to entities in direct contract with the named insured only. Commonly found on otherwise-good AI endorsements. Downstream subs or second-tier contractors get no coverage.

## 3. Height Exclusions
Manuscript endorsements barring coverage for work at heights. Common in major cities. Even worse: exclusions based on building height regardless of whether work is inside or outside.

## 4. Builder's Risk Exclusions on CGL Policies
Builder's risk is first-party — it won't fund the insured's defense. If the CGL has a builder's risk exclusion, the insured pays their own attorney to defend a construction defect suit.

## 5. Sloppy Naming of Insureds
"ABC Contracting, et al." — the "et al." is not reliable coverage for related entities. If XYZ Contractor has a major loss, "et al." won't save them.

## 6. Scope of Work Exclusions
Exclusions for the precise scope of work the contractor performs. Roofing contractor with a total roofing exclusion. Often appears on policies for contractors keeping costs low — or who didn't tell their broker what they actually do.

## 7. Scope of Work Exclusions in Disguise
Same as #6 but hidden as "classification limitations." If a drywall sub's coverage is limited to the classification listed in the dec page, and dec page only lists "masonry," they have zero drywall coverage.

## 8. Missing Endorsements on Excess Policies
Excess policies are widely misunderstood to automatically follow form for P&NC. They don't. If the excess policy is not specifically endorsed for primary/noncontributory, it will not respond before the AI's own primary coverage — 99% of the time.

## 9. Over-Broad Wrap-Up Exclusions
"Projects in which you are involved" — even if the sub is excluded from a wrap, their own policy may not respond either. Also: endorsements that bar coverage whenever ANY project-specific policy is in place, even one that doesn't extend to subs.

## 10. Fraudulent Endorsements
Doctored AI endorsements with handwritten additions. One real example: a schedule that read "the action over exclusion has been removed from the policy" — handwritten, ineffective, and submitted as real.
